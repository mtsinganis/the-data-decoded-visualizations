A New Methodology for Estimation of Israelis’ International Migration by
the Israel Central Bureau of Statistics (ICBS)1
Written by: Eilat Cohen-Kastro, Senior Department of Demography and
Census, ICBS
preface
International migration constitutes a principal part of the national statistics of
most countries. Among the important tasks undertaken by the Israel Central
Bureau of Statistics (ICBS) is definition and measurement of the population of
migrating Israelis, in order to prepare population estimates, define the
population of the Population Census, create sampling frames of populations for
various surveys, and remove Israelis who emigrate from administrative files.
Additionally, the migrant population is of great public and academic interest,
resulting in a high demand for data and research on the subject.
In order to fulfil the required tasks and also maintain high statistical
quality, the ICBS needs to produce reliable, homogeneous estimates of Israelis’
international migration. In order to improve the national statistics on the subject
in accordance with global changes in migration patterns and with technological
advances, the ICBS set out to update the definitions of Israelis included in
international migration statistics as well as upgrading to monthly estimates.
This methodological paper presents the background and rationale for
developing the new methodology for measuring Israelis' international migration,
as well as presenting the new definitions and data sources. Also presented are
the limitations of the data, the new annual estimates, and a comparison with
the former methodology (pre-change). It was found that the estimate of long-
term Israeli emigrants is significantly higher using the new methodology (post-
change), averaging 2.4 times higher than while using the former methodology.
The estimate of returning Israelis was also found to be significantly higher using
the new methodology, averaging 2.7 times higher than the estimate of returning
Israelis using the former methodology. Additionally, the differences between
population estimates and population growth rates calculated according to the
two methodologies are presented.
Keywords: Emigration from Israel, International migration, Departing Israelis,
Returning Israelis, Migration patterns, International migration balance,
Population growth components, ICBS.

Introduction
Reliable estimation of the extent of Israelis' international migration is crucial to
the quality of the statistics produced by the Israel Central Bureau of Statistics
(ICBS). International migration estimates play an important role in the CBS's
fields of activity. Our work includes determining the de jure population living in
Israel, for the following purposes: calculating population estimates; producing
indices, samples, and statistical models; identifying and indicating the
population not residing in Israel in administrative files from non-ICBS sources;
and characterizing and researching various aspects of the population in Israel
and the one emigrating from it.
Estimation of the migrants and their characteristics should reflect as
much as possible the changing reality of migration patterns, and the extent to
which trends among departing and returning Israelis can be identified and
changes in their characteristics can be monitored in detail. In actuality, there is
no single "correct" definition of who is a migrant from Israel, because (a) there
is no survey that asks every departing or returning Israeli the purpose of their
trip and (b) inaccuracies would certainly still occur as initial migration decisions
can change with time. Therefore, the ICBS aims to adopt a demographic
definition of emigration that includes as much of the population as possible who
– with a reasonable level of certainty – moved their centre of life abroad and
which, conversely, includes as little as possible of the population who did not.
Due to these considerations, the ICBS decided to develop a new, updated
methodology for estimation of international migration that would better meet the
needs of national statistics. The results of this developmental process are
presented in this article.
The aim of this methodological article is to compare the two
methodologies (former and new) and to present the implications of the new
methodology on population growth estimates. This is accomplished by
presenting the methodology formerly used in the Population Sector of the ICBS
for estimation of migrating Israelis, describing the rationale for updating it,
followed by a presentation of the new methodology. Additionally, the data
sources and the limitations of the new methodology are presented. The Results
chapter presents the series of estimates obtained from using the new
methodology and compares them to the estimates obtained using the former
methodology. Additionally, the impact of implementing the new methodology on
population estimates and on the rate of population growth will be examined.

Current Methodology for Estimating International Migration Among
Israelis
The main methodology for calculating the flow of Israelis' migration movements
has been used in its former form for several decades in the Demography Sector
of the ICBS. The definitions guiding it have been in effect since 1995. The
definitions of departing and returning Israelis were not symmetrical; that is, the
number of days required to stay abroad for departing Israelis differs from the
number of days required to stay in Israel for returning Israelis. Departing
Israelis were defined as Israelis who stayed abroad 365 days or more
continuously, and who before departing stayed at least ninety days
continuously in Israel. Returning Israelis were defined as Israelis who stayed
abroad 365 days or more continuously before entering Israel, and who after
their return stayed at least ninety days continuously in Israel. In consequence,
these definitions do not include Israelis residing abroad who visit their homeland
for a short period (less than three months continuously) (CBS, 2023).
Since 2008, the source of data regarding departing and returning Israelis
has been registration in the Border Control Registration system, which is
conducted by border inspectors under the auspices of the Population and
Immigration Authority. Border inspection stations are located at all entry/exit
points on Israel's borders by air, land, and sea. In the past, when a
resident/citizen exited or entered Israel, border inspectors documented the
action by manually entering the person's information into the Border Control
Registration system. Starting from 2010, passport information (without manual
entry) has been scanned at Ben Gurion Airport, and by 2013, the scanning
method was in use at all border stations in Israel except for a few border
crossing points. Scanning is intended to reduce manual entry errors and
thereby improve data quality. This system includes records of all exits/entries,
the associated personal identification numbers, and the exit/entry dates. This
data accumulates in statistical data files that are later completed with additional
personal information from the Population Register: sex, date of birth, religion,
date of immigration, marital status, and country of birth. The innovation since
2008 is that these records are transferred directly from the Border Control
Registration system to the ICBS migration data system, and thus need less
human intervention. The migration data system aggregates all movements of
Israelis (including movements of East Jerusalem Arabs passing through
international border crossing points, but excluding crossings between Israel
and Palestinian Authority areas) at the borders in a calendar year (from January
1 to December 31 each year). The former methodology calculated the length of
an Israeli’s stay abroad and their length of stay in Israel before departing. The
system also performed the same calculations on the records of Israelis who

enter Israel. Calculation of departing flow (out-flow) and return flow (in-flow) is
based on border movements only.
The products of the former system were two annual files: one for
departing Israelis and one for returning Israelis (CBS, 2014b). In the ICBS's
annual Statistical Abstract of Israel, annual data on Israeli residents who stayed
abroad for 365 days or more continuously, and the returning Israelis from
among these persons who stayed in Israel for at least ninety days continuously,
are published according to sex and age. The ICBS also publishes a media
release each year t entitled "Departures and Returns in [year] (e.g., 2020) of
Israelis Staying Abroad Continuously for a Year or More", which contains
estimates for year t-2 of the number of departing and returning Israelis, their
demographic characteristics, and an estimate of the stock of Israelis residing
outside Israel.
Motivations for Updating the former Methodology
In recent years, there has been growing recognition within the ICBS's Senior
Department of Demography and Census of the need to update the statistics of
international migration through the development of an up-to-date methodology
that is more suitable for its aims. In particular, the need arose to update the
definitions of departing and returning Israelis such that these reflect the
changing reality of migration patterns in the present times. These patterns are
influenced by the increased availability and decreasing costs of international
travel, the rapidly developing connections of Israel to the global economic
market, increased business opportunities abroad, the development of the IT
sector leading to job opportunities requiring relocation and study abroad, and
the expansion of the dual citizenship phenomenon, along with an Israeli society
characterized by close familial and social ties.
These factors have led, on one hand, to an increase in the number of
departing Israelis and, on the other hand, to an increased tendency to return
for visits to Israel. Therefore, the former methodology, which requires a stay
abroad of a full continuous year (if this sequence is 'broken' by a visit to Israel,
the person will not be considered an Israeli emigrant), is less suited to
contemporary migration patterns. Additionally, there is no usage of the stock of
Israelis residing abroad in determining whether a person crossing the border
belongs to the out-flow or the in-flow migration movements. This can lead to a
person being registered as a departing Israeli twice without being counted as a
returning Israeli between one departure and the next). Moreover, there is no
integration of information regarding the person's civil status in the Population
Register, as a result of which persons registered in the Population Register who
have never resided in Israel might be included in the estimates as migrants.

A team was formed within the ICBS's Senior Department of Demography
and Census which aim was to conducted a research and based upon to
formulate recommendations for improving and updating the handling of
international migration statistics. The recommendations of the team were (1) to
establish one comprehensive "emigrant" stock to serve all of the ICBS’s needs,
and (2) that this stock should include two main components: (a) a list of Israelis
(stock) at a specific point in time (an exact date) who are highly likely not to
belong to the population of Israel at that time; and (b) a multi-year stock of flows
including a list of people who emigrated and/or returned, with information
necessary for managing their movements. The definitions of departing and
returning Israelis would take into account short visits in Israel, and would
provide information about short-term and long-term emigrants. The operational
distinction between the needs of the Population Census and the needs of the
Demography Sector were preserved in the recommendations.
At the beginning of the development of the new methodology, five
alternatives for defining departing Israelis and returning Israelis were
researched, differing in various combinations of three guiding principles: (a) the
existence or non-existence of a required initial duration of continuous stay
abroad; (b) the cumulative number of days spent abroad; and (c) maintaining
symmetry or allowing for asymmetry in the definitions of departing and returning
Israelis. The alternatives were examined based on data from border crossings
and calculations of Israelis' stays in Israel and abroad in a closed population
(without taking into account natural increase or the arrival of "olim" (immigrants
to Israel under the Law of Return) for 2009–2013. The characteristics of the
Israelis included in each alternative were examined through use of their
demographic and socio-economic characteristics.
The findings of the research recommended the methodological
alternative presented later in this paper, which implements the goal of including
as much as possible the population that has likely relocated its centre of life
outside of Israel and excluding as much as possible the population that has not
done so, compared to other alternatives. Furthermore, this alternative supports
the need for an initial period of a continuous stay of ninety days abroad, while
allowing for an additional period of cumulative days abroad, totalling at least
nine months out of the twelve months since departing. In addition, this
alternative maintains symmetry between the definitions of those departing and
those returning. The research findings and UN recommendations were adopted
by the advisory committee on demographic issues of the Public Advisory
Council for Statistics, which included demography experts headed by Dr.
Eliyahu Ben-Moshe (chairman of the advisory committee) and the former
National Statistician Professor Danny Pfeffermann, and the development of the
chosen methodology was initiated.

The New Methodology for Estimating International Migration Among
Israelis
The population included in the new methodology was determined according to
the definitions of the de jure population, upon which population estimates have
been based by the ICBS since the 2008 census. The de jure population
includes the total number of Israelis registered in the Population Register
having the status of citizen, permanent resident, or temporary resident.
However, there are persons registered in the Population Register who have
never resided in Israel for an extended period, such as immigrating citizens (a
person born to an Israeli citizen while residing abroad). These persons should
not be included in estimates of departing Israelis because they were never part
of the de jure population in Israel in the first place. These immigrating citizens,
as well as new registrants in the Population Register with an initial status of not
an active resident are not included in the estimate of the flow of departing
Israelis, but are included in the gross stock of Israelis staying abroad (detailed
explanation of the stock is provided later in the paper).
The new definitions established are as follows:
Israeli emigrants: An Israeli who stayed abroad for at least 9 months
cumulatively during the year following their departure, of which the first 3
months were continuous.
Returning Israeli immigrants: An Israeli who stayed in Israel for at least 9
months cumulatively during the year following their entry, of which the first 3
months were continuous. This indicates an Israeli who returns to live in Israel
after emigrating.
These definitions are based on establishing a transfer of one's centre of life and
on a substantive cumulative stay abroad versus measurement of just
continuous residence in a foreign country. They are measured symmetrically
for departing and returning Israelis, thus providing a uniform, equal-opportunity
criterion for inclusion in the migration flows and preventing multiple or conflicting
classifications. Additionally, the initial required threshold of ninety continuous
days abroad or in Israel serves to distinguish between a tourist and a migrant
(a regular tourist visa is usually granted for a period of up to ninety days).
In addition to the updates of definitions for Israeli emigrants and
Returning Israeli immigrants, we created two additional types of migration flows
that did not exist previously: Short-term emigrants, referring to persons who
stayed abroad for between 3 months (initially and continuously) and up to 9
months (cumulatively) during the year following departure. Visiting Israelis
refers to Israelis residing permanently abroad who came for a relatively
prolonged visit in Israel, staying in Israel for between 3 months (initially and

continuously) and 9 months(cumulatively) during the year following entry. In
addition to the migration flows mentioned above, the first population stock of
Israelis residing abroad was created.
The stock of Israelis residing abroad is based on the results of the
Population and Housing Census 2008, which determined who belonged to the
population residing in Israel and who did not, at the end of 2008. From 2009
onward, the stock is updated according to the migration flows into and out of
the country, and according to population status updates as recorded in the
Population Register. Contributions to the stock include: (a) Israeli emigrants
(see above definition) and (b) Israelis registering for the first time in the
Population Register whose status is " not active", or who are "active" but have
never resided in Israel for a long period of time. Removal of records from the
stock include: (a) Returning Israeli immigrants (see above definition), (b) Death
notifications of persons included in the stock as they are recorded by the
Population Register, or (c) persons included in the stock reaching the age of
110, whose death was not officially reported. This gross stock allows
administrative indication for each person in the Population Register of whether,
or not, they are part of the population residing permanently in Israel at a certain
point in time. However, this stock is still an overestimation of the number of
Israelis emigrants living abroad, because it includes an unknown number of
deceased persons who have died aboard without their death being reported to
the Population Register; as well as children of Israeli citizens born abroad who
are registered in the Population Register without ever been permanent
residents of Israel.
Sources of Information for the New Methodology
The quality of international migration statistics derives from the quality of the
data sources available to formulate these statistics. The more comprehensive,
reliable, and up-to-date a data source is, the higher the quality of the data and
thus the ability of statisticians, decision-makers, researchers, and the general
public to rely on them.
The main data source for formulating the new methodology is the
border control system under the auspices of the Population and Immigration
Authority. Israel has a unique situation in that its borders are relatively
controlled and closed compared to other countries. In other words, people
cannot move freely between Israel and neighbouring countries without
registering their movement at land border crossing points (the situation differs
in most European countries, for example), and registration of movements at
non-land border crossings is done through airport and seaport crossing points.
This situation allows almost complete coverage and documentation of all
movements by residents across the borders. The data is gathered via automatic
7

computerized scanning of passports conducted at all border stations and
crossing checkpoints and transmitted directly to the ICBS.
The Population Register serves as a secondary data source. A
person's citizenship status upon their first registration in the Population Register
is obtained from this source. An initial registration of an Israeli in the Population
Register with a status of "not active" (according to the Population Authority's
discretion), or that of an immigrating citizen who resides abroad, indicates that
the person is not actually residing in Israel, but that there is a future possibility
that they will come to reside in Israel if they so wish. These persons are added
to the stock of Israelis residing abroad. In addition, the Population Register
serves as a source for receipt of notifications of the deaths of Israeli residents
living abroad, thus allowing their removal from the stock of Israelis staying
abroad. This source provides complete coverage of the new registration of
persons in the Population Register and partial coverage of death notification for
those residing abroad.
Data Limitations
There are several systemic limitations to the obtained data which might affect
the data quality, but their impact on the overall data quality is marginal. The
limitations are as follows:
• Use of a foreign passport when exiting or entering, for someone with
dual citizenship, might lead to incomplete registration of a person's
border movements. Since 2008, the new border control system should
prohibit Israeli passport holders from exiting or entering Israel using a
foreign passport. Thus, if a border crossing with a foreign passport
occurs, the system is programmed to link it to their Israeli identity
information. However, it could occur in some rare cases this linkage is
not successful.
• If the death of an Israeli who was included in the stock of Israelis residing
abroad is not reported by the family to the Population Register in Israel
(probably more common in cases, in which there is no incentive or
benefit to report it), this person will not receive a status of deceased in
the Population Register, and therefore will not be removed from the
stock. Thus, the gross stock of residents abroad is an overestimate for
the number of Israelis residing abroad. A certain amount of correction is
done by removing anyone who has reached the age of 110 (by given the
status of "presumed deceased").
• It might be somewhat problematic that our determination of whether a
person is a departing Israeli, or a returning Israeli, is dependent on their
presence or lack thereof, in the stock of residents abroad. This applies
in cases in which the initial determination of place of residency in the
8

• 2008 census was erroneous. If so, the error will be carried over to future
years if the person does not cross any of the borders thereafter.
• Because the new migration definitions are general and are applied
equally and objectively to the entire population, without considering
individual travel patterns, it is possible to incorrectly classify persons as
non-emigrants even if they have relocated their centre of life abroad
(false negative). This situation can occur if the number of days spent by
a person abroad does not meet the definition set for Israeli emigrants,
and therefore they will not be classified as such. For example, persons
who consider themselves residents of another country, but who have
never stayed 3 months continuously abroad or who divide their time fairly
equal between abroad and Israel, will not be defined as emigrants under
the new definitions. The same might happen with returning Israeli
immigrants.
• The data include international border crossings of Israel for Israelis
(including East Jerusalem residents), and exclude crossings of Israelis
into the Judea and Samaria Area and the Gaza Strip, and vice versa.
Examining the impact of transitioning to the new methodology on
population growth and its estimates
In order to assess the extent of the impact of transitioning to the new
methodology on the components and rate of population growth, and thus on the
population estimates of Israel, the population estimate was calculated in two
different ways: (a) based on the components of growth under the former
methodology as published in the ICBS's annual Statistical Abstract; and
(b) based on the same components of growth (births, deaths, "olim" -
immigrants, immigrating citizens, and family reunification), excluding the
international migration balance component of Israelis, which was recalculated
according to the new methodology. The simulation was conducted for 2012–
2020. Additionally, it should be noted that the components of growth for 2020
were exceptional because they were influenced by the global COVID–19
pandemic, which affected persons’ ability and/or willingness to immigrate to and
emigrate from Israel, as well as resulting in a decrease in the number of births
and an increase in the number of deaths in that year. The following diagram
illustrates the results of the population calculations using both methods.

An analysis of the results shows that, as expected, the use of the new
methodology reduces the population estimate compared to the former
estimates. Of the population components, the natural increase balance
component accounts for approximately 83% on average annually for the years
2012–2020 and the international migration balance component accounts for
approximately 17% of the total annual population growth in the former
methodology. In contrast, in the new methodology, the natural increase balance
component increases to approximately 86% on average, and the international
migration balance component decreases to approximately 14% on average.
The contribution of the international migration balance component to population
growth decreases by 4% on average in the transition from the former to the new
methodology, and the contribution of the natural increase balance to the
population growth increases by an equal percentage.
Because the data on natural increase, immigrants, returning citizens,
and family reunification were held constant, the reduction in the population
estimate stems from the new definitions of departing and returning Israelis in
the new methodology. This mainly results from a larger increase in the number
of departing Israelis compared to a smaller increase in the number of returning
Israelis. The new, more flexible and symmetric definitions increase the negative
10

migration balance between out-flow and in-flow, which in turn reduces the
overall international migration balance of Israel, though it remains positive. The
reduction in the international migration balance is also reflected in the slowdown
of population growth under the new methodology; the population decreased by
approximately 0.1% per year compared to the growth rate under the former
methodology.
The decrease in the growth rate of the total population is not surprising
because of the broadening of the definitions of who is considered an Israeli
emigrant. This happens by allowing for cumulative stays abroad, which
translate into allowing visits to Israel during the first year after departing, rather
than requiring a stay of a consecutive year abroad. This in turn leads to a higher
estimate of departing Israelis, thus reducing the share of the international
migration balance component in the overall population growth of Israel. This
decrease in the total population growth rate is not dramatic because Israel's
population growth rate is mainly influenced by the natural increase.
Nevertheless, it should not be overlooked that there is a cumulative effect: The
decrease in the growth rate added up to approximately 4.2% cumulatively
during 2012–2020. Adding up all the differences between the negative
migration balance (more emigrating than returning Israelis) under the new
methodology compared to this negative balance under the former methodology
results in a cumulative population loss of approximately 57,500 persons for
2010–2020.
Summary
The paper highlights the need to update definitions of departing Israelis, by
allowing visits to Israel within the first year after departing abroad, and by
maintaining symmetry by using the same definitions among returning Israelis.
Numerous studies conducted by the ICBS indicate that updating the definitions
of departing and returning Israelis will help produce more reliable statistics
regarding the identification of Israelis and who moved their centre of life abroad
and those who decided to return to Israel. This will contribute to the calculation
of more reliable estimates of the population residing permanently in Israel which
will enable better-informed policy planning based on this.
Additional benefits of the new methodology include creating new
immigration statuses of short-term emigrants (from three to nine months
cumulative stay abroad) and of visiting Israelis (from three to nine months
cumulative stay in Israel), along with the existing statuses of long-term
emigrants and of returning Israelis. The symmetry between these definitions
facilitates comparability and eliminates the possibility of overlapping
immigration statuses in the same year. Furthermore, establishing a database
of Israelis residing abroad based on migration flows enables the identification

of persons registered in the Population Register who are currently residing
abroad at a specific point in time, thus enabling the classification of Israelis in
the Population Register into three primary groups: persons residing in Israel,
persons residing abroad, and deceased persons. The latter two groups should
be removed from calculations of population estimates, statistical indices, and
when sampling populations for surveys, as well as being removed from
administrative files received from data sources outside the ICBS.
