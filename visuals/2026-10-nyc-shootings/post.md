## Proposed copy

New York’s pandemic shooting surge was striking. So was the recovery. Any account of the city that explains only the first half is missing a pretty extraordinary second half.

The numbers:
• 2020: 1,532 shooting incidents
• 2021: 1,562
• 2025: 688
That’s a 56% decline from 2021—and the lowest annual total in the NYC Open Data series going back to 2006. NYPD’s longer records also identify 2025 as a record low.
New York didn’t just unwind the pandemic surge. It finished below its previous best year.
The monthly chart makes the disruption especially vivid. July 2020 recorded 243 shooting incidents, the highest monthly count in this series. December 2025 recorded 35, the lowest. Those months occupy different points in the seasonal cycle, but each was an extreme within two decades of observations.

What happened?

The surge came amid an enormous disruption to everyday life: schools and services closed, economic insecurity intensified, and relations between police and communities deteriorated. Gun violence rose across much of America, making explanations built around a single New York policy hard to sustain.

The decline deserves the same analytical discipline. Restored routines and social supports, focused enforcement, and community violence prevention are all plausible contributors. NYPD emphasizes targeting shooting hotspots, violent groups, and illegal guns. But a falling line cannot tell us how much credit each intervention deserves.

Here’s my takeaway: extrapolating a crisis into a permanent future is a bad forecasting habit.

2020–2021 showed how quickly public safety can deteriorate. The years that followed showed that the deterioration could be reversed—and that the pre-pandemic level wasn’t an unavoidable floor.

688 shootings is still an enormous human toll. Citywide progress also doesn’t mean every neighborhood shares equally in it.

But a 56% decline is consequential progress. It deserves both recognition and a serious effort to understand what made it possible.

Data: NYPD via NYC Open Data. Counts refer to shooting incidents, not people shot.
